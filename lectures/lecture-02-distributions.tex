\chapter{Distributions, Expectation, and Variance}
\label{chap:distributions}

A distribution describes how a random quantity varies. The useful starting
point is a mechanism: waiting for a request, counting successful trials, adding
small measurement errors, or sampling from a population whose members differ.
Expectation and variance let us compare these mechanisms and explain what
happens when their outputs are transformed, added, or mixed.

\section{Exponential waiting times}
\label{sec:exponential}

Suppose requests arrive at a service, and let \(T\) be the waiting time until
the next request. A simple model assumes a constant arrival rate
\(\lambda>0\). Over a short interval of length \(h\), conditional on still
waiting, the chance of an arrival is approximately \(\lambda h\), regardless
of how long we have already waited.

Write \(S(t)=\Prob(T>t)\), the \emph{survival function}. The constant-rate
assumption gives
\[
  S(t+h)=S(t)\bigl(1-\lambda h+o(h)\bigr),
\]
where \(o(h)/h\to0\) as \(h\to0\). Consequently,
\[
  S'(t)=-\lambda S(t),\qquad S(0)=1,
\]
whose solution is \(S(t)=e^{-\lambda t}\).

\begin{definition}[Exponential distribution]
The notation \(T\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)\) means that \(T\) has density
\[
  f_T(t)=
  \begin{cases}
    \lambda e^{-\lambda t},&t\ge0,\\
    0,&t<0.
  \end{cases}
\]
The parameter \(\lambda\) is a \emph{rate}: if \(t\) is in minutes,
\(\lambda\) is in inverse minutes.
\end{definition}

A density assigns probabilities by area:
\[
  \Prob(a<T\le b)=\int_a^b f_T(t)\,dt,\qquad
  \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}f_T(t)\,dt=1.
\]
It is not a point probability: \(\Prob(T=t)=0\) for every fixed \(t\).
Density values can exceed one; their integral over an interval cannot.

The \emph{cumulative distribution function} (CDF) of any random variable is
\(F_X(x)=\Prob(X\le x)\). In this example,
\[
  F_T(t)=
  \begin{cases}
    0,&t<0,\\
    1-e^{-\lambda t},&t\ge0.
  \end{cases}
\]
Every CDF is nondecreasing and right-continuous, tends to zero at
\(-\infty\) and to one at \(+\infty\), and satisfies
\[
  \Prob(a<X\le b)=F_X(b)-F_X(a).
\]
For a variable with density, \(F_X(x)=\int_{-\infty}^x f_X(u)\,du\);
where this function is differentiable, \(F_X'(x)=f_X(x)\).

\begin{example}[Waiting for a request]
Let \(\lambda=0.2\) per minute. Then
\[
  \Prob(T>10)=e^{-2}\approx0.1353,\qquad
  \Prob(2<T\le5)=e^{-0.4}-e^{-1}\approx0.3024.
\]
The \(q\)-quantile, the time by which an arrival occurs with probability
\(q\in(0,1)\), is
\[
  t_q=-\frac{\log(1-q)}{\lambda}.
\]
In particular, \(t_{0.95}\approx14.98\) minutes.
\end{example}

\subsection{Memorylessness and its limits}

For \(s,t\ge0\),
\[
  \Prob(T>s+t\mid T>s)
  =\frac{e^{-\lambda(s+t)}}{e^{-\lambda s}}
  =e^{-\lambda t}
  =\Prob(T>t).
\]
After waiting \(s\) units of time without an arrival, the remaining waiting
time has the original distribution. This is \emph{memorylessness}.
It is an assumption about the mechanism: equipment that ages, scheduled
arrivals, and demand that changes throughout the day need different models.

If \(c>0\), then \(cT\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda/c)\), since
\(\Prob(cT>t)=e^{-(\lambda/c)t}\). Changing the time unit changes the
numerical rate.

\section{Expectation and functions of a random variable}
\label{sec:expectation}

\begin{definition}[Expectation]
For a discrete random variable with probability mass function (PMF)
\(p_X(x)=\Prob(X=x)\), define
\[
  \Expect[X]=\sum_x x\,p_X(x).
\]
For a random variable with density \(f_X\), define
\[
  \Expect[X]=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}x f_X(x)\,dx.
\]
A finite expectation is guaranteed when \(\Expect[|X|]<\infty\), meaning
that the corresponding sum or integral of absolute values is finite.
\end{definition}

A PMF consists of nonnegative masses summing to one. A density consists of
nonnegative heights integrating to one. Expectation is a probability-weighted
average in either case. It need not be a possible observation: a fair
six-sided die has expectation \(3.5\).

For \(T\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)\), integration by parts gives
\begin{align*}
  \Expect[T]
  &=\int_0^\infty t\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\,dt\\
  &=\left[-t e^{-\lambda t}\right]_0^\infty
    +\int_0^\infty e^{-\lambda t}\,dt
   =\frac1\lambda.
\end{align*}
Thus a rate of \(0.2\) per minute corresponds to a mean waiting time of
five minutes. The rate and the mean waiting time are reciprocals.

\subsection{Computing the expectation of a function}

For a measurable function \(g\), provided \(\Expect[|g(X)|]<\infty\),
\begin{equation}
  \Expect[g(X)]
  =
  \begin{cases}
    \displaystyle\sum_x g(x)p_X(x),&X\text{ discrete},\\[6pt]
    \displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}g(x)f_X(x)\,dx,
      &X\text{ has a density}.
  \end{cases}
  \label{eq:expectation-function}
\end{equation}
There is no need to find the distribution of \(g(X)\) first.

\begin{example}[A nonlinear function of waiting time]
Suppose a waiting time \(T\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)\) receives a
discount factor \(e^{-aT}\), where \(a>0\). Then
\[
  \Expect[e^{-aT}]
  =\int_0^\infty e^{-at}\lambda e^{-\lambda t}\,dt
  =\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+a}.
\]
This differs from \(e^{-a\Expect[T]}=e^{-a/\lambda}\).
In general, \(\Expect[g(X)]\ne g(\Expect[X])\).
\end{example}

Taking \(g(x)=x^r\) gives the \(r\)-th raw moment. For a positive integer
\(r\), repeated integration by parts yields
\[
  \Expect[T^r]=\frac{r}{\lambda}\Expect[T^{r-1}]
  =\frac{r!}{\lambda^r}.
\]
In particular, \(\Expect[T^2]=2/\lambda^2\).

\subsection{Linearity}

\begin{theorem}[Linearity of expectation]
Let \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) have finite expectations.
For any constants \(a_i\) and \(b\),
\[
  \Expect\!\left[b+\sum_{i=1}^n a_iX_i\right]
  =b+\sum_{i=1}^n a_i\Expect[X_i].
\]
Independence is not required.
\end{theorem}

For a finite sample space, the reason is the distributive law:
\[
  \sum_{\omega\in\Omega}
    \bigl(aX(\omega)+bY(\omega)\bigr)\Prob(\{\omega\})
  =a\Expect[X]+b\Expect[Y].
\]
The same argument extends to integrable random variables using linearity of
integration.

For an event \(A\), its indicator \(\mathbf1_A\) equals one on \(A\) and zero
otherwise, so \(\Expect[\mathbf1_A]=\Prob(A)\). Therefore the expected
number of events occurring among \(A_1,\ldots,A_n\) is
\[
  \Expect\!\left[\sum_{i=1}^n\mathbf1_{A_i}\right]
  =\sum_{i=1}^n\Prob(A_i),
\]
even if the events depend on one another.

Linearity does not extend to arbitrary operations. For example,
\(\Expect[XY]=\Expect[X]\Expect[Y]\) holds for independent integrable
\(X,Y\), but need not hold for dependent variables.

\section{Variance and standard deviation}
\label{sec:variance}

\begin{definition}[Variance]
If \(\Expect[X^2]<\infty\), the variance and standard deviation of \(X\) are
\[
  \Var(X)=\Expect\!\left[(X-\Expect[X])^2\right],
  \qquad
  \operatorname{sd}(X)=\sqrt{\Var(X)}.
\]
\end{definition}

Variance measures squared spread around the mean. It is nonnegative, and
equals zero exactly when \(X\) equals a constant with probability one.
If \(X\) is measured in seconds, its variance is in seconds squared,
while its standard deviation is in seconds.

Expanding the square and applying linearity gives the computational identity
\begin{equation}
  \Var(X)=\Expect[X^2]-\Expect[X]^2.
  \label{eq:variance-moments}
\end{equation}
For exponential waiting times,
\[
  \Var(T)=\frac{2}{\lambda^2}-\frac{1}{\lambda^2}
  =\frac{1}{\lambda^2},
  \qquad \operatorname{sd}(T)=\frac1\lambda.
\]
The mean and standard deviation are equal: exponential waiting times have
substantial variation relative to their average.

\subsection{Shifting, scaling, and adding}

For constants \(a,b\),
\[
  \Expect[aX+b]=a\Expect[X]+b,\qquad
  \Var(aX+b)=a^2\Var(X).
\]
A shift changes the mean but leaves the spread unchanged. Multiplication by
\(a\) changes standard deviation by \(|a|\), and variance by \(a^2\).
Variance is not a linear operation.

For random variables with finite second moments, define
\[
  \Cov(X,Y)
  =\Expect\!\left[(X-\Expect[X])(Y-\Expect[Y])\right].
\]
Expanding the square of the sum gives
\begin{equation}
  \Var(X+Y)=\Var(X)+\Var(Y)+2\Cov(X,Y).
  \label{eq:variance-sum}
\end{equation}
If \(X,Y\) are independent, their covariance is zero. Consequently, for
independent \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\),
\[
  \Var\!\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_iX_i\right)
  =\sum_{i=1}^n a_i^2\Var(X_i).
\]
Pairwise zero covariance is sufficient; it does not in general imply
independence.

\begin{example}[Why the independence condition matters]
Suppose \(X\) and \(Y\) each have variance one. If they are independent,
\(\Var(X+Y)=2\). If \(Y=X\), the variance is four. If \(Y=-X\), it is zero.
Knowing the individual variances alone does not determine the variance of
the sum.
\end{example}

\section{Poisson counts}
\label{sec:poisson}

Let \(W_1,W_2,\ldots\) be independent \(\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)\)
waiting times, and write
\[
  T_0=0,\qquad T_k=W_1+\cdots+W_k.
\]
The \(T_k\) are arrival times. The number of arrivals during \((0,t]\) is
\[
  N(t)=\max\{k\ge0:T_k\le t\}.
\]
This construction gives a \emph{homogeneous Poisson process}: counts in
disjoint intervals are independent, and the count distribution depends only
on interval length. In particular, \(N(t)\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda t)\).

\begin{definition}[Poisson distribution]
A count \(N\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(m)\), with \(m>0\), has PMF
\[
  \Prob(N=k)=e^{-m}\frac{m^k}{k!},
  \qquad k=0,1,2,\ldots.
\]
The parameter \(m\) is the expected count in the observation interval.
For a process with rate \(\lambda\) observed for time \(t\), \(m=\lambda t\).
\end{definition}

Normalization follows from the exponential series:
\[
  \sum_{k=0}^\infty e^{-m}\frac{m^k}{k!}=e^{-m}e^m=1.
\]
Shifting the summation index gives the mean,
\[
  \Expect[N]
  =\sum_{k=1}^\infty k e^{-m}\frac{m^k}{k!}
  =m\sum_{j=0}^\infty e^{-m}\frac{m^j}{j!}=m.
\]
The same method gives \(\Expect[N(N-1)]=m^2\). Since
\(N^2=N(N-1)+N\),
\[
  \Expect[N^2]=m^2+m,\qquad \Var(N)=m.
\]
The equality of mean and variance is a specific property of the Poisson
model, not a general property of count data.

\begin{example}[Counts and waiting times in the same service]
With rate \(\lambda=0.2\) per minute, the ten-minute count has \(m=2\).
Thus
\[
  \Prob(N(10)=0)=e^{-2},\qquad
  \Prob(N(10)\ge2)=1-e^{-2}(1+2)\approx0.5940.
\]
The equality \(\Prob(N(10)=0)=\Prob(T_1>10)\) expresses the same event
using a count or a waiting time.
\end{example}

Independent Poisson counts add:
\[
  N_1\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(m_1),\quad
  N_2\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(m_2)
  \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
  N_1+N_2\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(m_1+m_2).
\]
Indeed, summing over \(N_1=j\) and using the binomial theorem gives
\[
  \Prob(N_1+N_2=k)
  =\frac{e^{-(m_1+m_2)}}{k!}
    \sum_{j=0}^k\binom{k}{j}m_1^j m_2^{k-j}
  =\frac{e^{-(m_1+m_2)}(m_1+m_2)^k}{k!}.
\]
Bursts, dependence between arrivals, or varying rates can make a single
homogeneous Poisson model unsuitable.

\section{Gamma waiting times and the event-count connection}
\label{sec:gamma}

The time \(T_k\) until the \(k\)-th arrival is a sum of \(k\) independent
exponential variables. For \(k=2\), its density is obtained by convolution:
\[
  f_{T_2}(t)
  =\int_0^t \lambda e^{-\lambda u}
      \lambda e^{-\lambda(t-u)}\,du
  =\lambda^2 t e^{-\lambda t},\qquad t\ge0.
\]
Here convolution integrates over all ways the two waiting times can add to
\(t\). Repeating the argument gives
\[
  f_{T_k}(t)
  =\frac{\lambda^k}{(k-1)!}t^{k-1}e^{-\lambda t},
  \qquad t>0.
\]

\begin{definition}[Gamma distribution: shape and rate]
For \(\alpha,\beta>0\), the distribution
\(X\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)\) has density
\[
  f_X(x)=\frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}
      x^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta x},\qquad x>0,
\]
and zero density for \(x<0\). The gamma function is
\[
  \Gamma(\alpha)=\int_0^\infty u^{\alpha-1}e^{-u}\,du.
\]
The parameters \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are the \emph{shape} and
\emph{rate}, respectively.
\end{definition}

The value of a density at the single point zero is irrelevant to its
probabilities; for \(\alpha<1\), this density diverges as \(x\downarrow0\)
but remains integrable.
Integration by parts gives
\(\Gamma(\alpha+1)=\alpha\Gamma(\alpha)\), and \(\Gamma(1)=1\).
Thus \(\Gamma(k)=(k-1)!\) for positive integers \(k\).
The exponential is the case \(\alpha=1\), and
\[
  T_k\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(k,\lambda).
\]
The density is also well-defined for noninteger \(\alpha\), although the
literal \(k\)-th-arrival interpretation requires an integer shape.

Using the substitution \(u=\beta x\), we obtain
\[
  \Expect[X^r]
  =\frac{\Gamma(\alpha+r)}{\Gamma(\alpha)\beta^r},
  \qquad r>-\alpha.
\]
Taking \(r=1,2\) yields
\[
  \Expect[X]=\frac{\alpha}{\beta},\qquad
  \Expect[X^2]=\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)}{\beta^2},\qquad
  \Var(X)=\frac{\alpha}{\beta^2}.
\]
For integer shape these results also follow by adding the means and
variances of independent exponential waiting times.

\begin{warning}[Rate versus scale]
Some sources use \(\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha,\theta)\) with
\emph{scale} \(\theta=1/\beta\). Then the mean is \(\alpha\theta\)
and the variance is \(\alpha\theta^2\).
Always check the density, rather than inferring the convention from the
symbol used for the second parameter.
\end{warning}

\subsection{Two descriptions of the same event}

For \(k\ge1\),
\begin{equation}
  \{T_k\le t\}=\{N(t)\ge k\}.
  \label{eq:arrivals-counts}
\end{equation}
Having waited long enough for the \(k\)-th arrival means having counted at
least \(k\) arrivals. Consequently,
\[
  \Prob(T_k\le t)
  =1-e^{-\lambda t}\sum_{j=0}^{k-1}\frac{(\lambda t)^j}{j!}.
\]
Differentiating this expression recovers the Gamma density above.
Equivalently, subtracting the CDFs of \(T_k\) and \(T_{k+1}\) gives the
Poisson probability \(\Prob(N(t)=k)\).

With rate \(0.2\) per minute, the waiting time for three requests has mean
\(15\) minutes and variance \(75\) minutes squared. The probability of
receiving the third request within ten minutes is
\[
  \Prob(T_3\le10)
  =\Prob(N(10)\ge3)
  =1-e^{-2}(1+2+2^2/2)\approx0.3233.
\]

\section{Bernoulli and Binomial distributions}
\label{sec:bernoulli-binomial}

\subsection{One binary outcome}

If an event \(A\) occurs with probability \(p\), its indicator
\(B=\mathbf1_A\) follows a \emph{Bernoulli distribution},
\(B\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)\), where \(0\le p\le1\):
\[
  \Prob(B=1)=p,\qquad \Prob(B=0)=1-p.
\]
Examples include a successful transmission, a user clicking a link, or an
item failing inspection. Because \(B^2=B\),
\[
  \Expect[B]=p,\qquad
  \Expect[B^2]=p,\qquad
  \Var(B)=p(1-p).
\]
Variance is largest at \(p=1/2\), and is zero when the outcome is certain.

\subsection{Counting successes in a fixed number of trials}

Let \(B_1,\ldots,B_n\) be independent Bernoulli variables with the same
success probability \(p\). Their sum
\[
  S_n=B_1+\cdots+B_n\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,p)
\]
counts the successes. For \(0<p<1\),
\[
  \Prob(S_n=k)=\binom nk p^k(1-p)^{n-k},
  \qquad k=0,\ldots,n.
\]
There are \(\binom nk=n!/[k!(n-k)!]\) ways to locate the \(k\) successes,
and each such sequence has probability \(p^k(1-p)^{n-k}\).
At \(p=0\) or \(p=1\), the count is deterministically zero or \(n\).

Linearity and independence give
\[
  \Expect[S_n]=np,\qquad \Var(S_n)=np(1-p).
\]
For the proportion \(\widehat p=S_n/n\),
\[
  \Expect[\widehat p]=p,\qquad
  \Var(\widehat p)=\frac{p(1-p)}{n}.
\]
For these independent, identically distributed trials, averaging divides
the variance by \(n\). Dependence can change this conclusion quantitatively.

\begin{example}[Clicks among impressions]
Under an independent model with \(n=100\) impressions and click probability
\(p=0.03\), the click count has mean \(3\) and variance \(2.91\).
The probability of no clicks is
\[
  \Prob(S_{100}=0)=0.97^{100}\approx0.0476.
\]
Repeated impressions to the same user need not be independent; different
users need not have the same click probability.
\end{example}

\subsection{The rare-event limit}

Let \(p_n=m/n\), with \(m>0\) fixed. For each fixed nonnegative integer \(k\),
\begin{align*}
  \Prob(S_n=k)
  &=\binom nk\left(\frac mn\right)^k
      \left(1-\frac mn\right)^{n-k}\\
  &\longrightarrow \frac{m^k}{k!}e^{-m}.
\end{align*}
Thus \(\operatorname{Binomial}(n,m/n)\) approaches
\(\operatorname{Poisson}(m)\): many trials, each with a small success
probability, produce a Poisson count. Their moments agree in the limit:
\[
  np_n=m,\qquad np_n(1-p_n)\longrightarrow m.
\]
In the click example, the Poisson approximation gives a no-click
probability \(e^{-3}\approx0.0498\).

\section{Geometric waiting times}
\label{sec:geometric}

Instead of fixing the number of trials, keep performing independent
Bernoulli trials until the first success. Let \(G\) count the trials,
including the successful one. For \(0<p\le1\),
\[
  G\sim\operatorname{Geometric}(p),\qquad G\in\{1,2,\ldots\}.
\]
For \(0<p<1\),
\begin{align*}
  \Prob(G=k)&=(1-p)^{k-1}p,\qquad k=1,2,\ldots,\\
  \Prob(G>k)&=(1-p)^k,\qquad k=0,1,\ldots.
\end{align*}
For \(p=1\), \(G=1\) with probability one.
The alternative convention counts failures, \(H=G-1\), and has support
\(\{0,1,\ldots\}\).

Put \(q=1-p\). Differentiating the geometric series
\(\sum_{k=0}^\infty q^k=(1-q)^{-1}\), valid for \(|q|<1\), gives
\[
  \sum_{k=1}^\infty kq^{k-1}=\frac1{(1-q)^2},\qquad
  \sum_{k=2}^\infty k(k-1)q^{k-2}=\frac2{(1-q)^3}.
\]
Therefore
\[
  \Expect[G]=\frac1p,\qquad
  \Expect[G(G-1)]=\frac{2(1-p)}{p^2},\qquad
  \Var(G)=\frac{1-p}{p^2}.
\]
The failures count \(H\) has mean \((1-p)/p\) and the same variance.

Memorylessness follows directly from the tail probabilities:
\[
  \Prob(G>m+k\mid G>m)=(1-p)^k=\Prob(G>k).
\]
For \(0<p<1\), this holds for all nonnegative integers \(m,k\).
This is the discrete counterpart of exponential memorylessness.

\subsection{From trials to continuous time}

Suppose each trial lasts \(\Delta\) time units and succeeds with probability
\(p_\Delta=\lambda\Delta\), where \(0<\lambda\Delta<1\). Then
\[
  \Prob(\Delta G>t)
  =(1-\lambda\Delta)^{\lfloor t/\Delta\rfloor}
  \longrightarrow e^{-\lambda t}
  \qquad(\Delta\downarrow0).
\]
The rescaled geometric waiting time approaches
\(\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)\). Its moments also converge:
\[
  \Expect[\Delta G]=\frac1\lambda,\qquad
  \Var(\Delta G)=\frac{1-\lambda\Delta}{\lambda^2}
  \longrightarrow\frac1{\lambda^2}.
\]
The factor \(\Delta\) is essential: a number of trials becomes a time only
after multiplication by the duration of a trial.

\begin{example}[A simple scrolling model]
If a user independently stops after each video with probability \(p=0.1\),
the number of videos viewed has mean \(10\), variance \(90\), and
\(\Prob(G>20)=0.9^{20}\approx0.1216\).
If stopping becomes more likely as the user gets tired, memorylessness no
longer holds.
\end{example}

\section{The Gaussian distribution}
\label{sec:gaussian}

\begin{definition}[Gaussian or normal distribution]
For \(\mu\in\Real\) and \(\sigma>0\), the notation
\(X\sim\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) means
\[
  f_X(x)=\frac1{\sigma\sqrt{2\pi}}
    \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x-\mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}\right),
  \qquad x\in\Real.
\]
The mean is \(\mu\), and the variance is \(\sigma^2\).
\end{definition}

The Gaussian is symmetric around \(\mu\). Its support is the entire real
line, so it can assign probability to negative values even when the
observed quantity, such as a duration, cannot be negative.

\subsection{Normalization and moments}

Let \(I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-z^2/2}\,dz\). Squaring the integral and
switching to polar coordinates gives
\[
  I^2
  =\int_{\Real^2}e^{-(x^2+y^2)/2}\,dx\,dy
  =\int_0^{2\pi}\int_0^\infty e^{-r^2/2}r\,dr\,d\theta
  =2\pi.
\]
This explains the normalizing constant \(\sqrt{2\pi}\).
For \(Z\sim\mathcal N(0,1)\), write
\(\phi(z)=(2\pi)^{-1/2}e^{-z^2/2}\).
Symmetry gives \(\Expect[Z]=0\). Since \(\phi'(z)=-z\phi(z)\),
integration by parts yields
\[
  \Expect[Z^2]
  =-\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}z\phi'(z)\,dz
  =[-z\phi(z)]_{-\infty}^{\infty}
     +\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\phi(z)\,dz
  =1.
\]
Writing \(X=\mu+\sigma Z\) now gives
\[
  \Expect[X]=\mu,\qquad \Var(X)=\sigma^2.
\]
The second argument of \(\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)\) is variance, not
standard deviation.

\subsection{Standardization and interval probabilities}

The transformation
\[
  Z=\frac{X-\mu}{\sigma}\sim\mathcal N(0,1)
\]
converts a Gaussian variable to the standard normal. Define
\[
  \Phi(z)=\int_{-\infty}^z\phi(u)\,du.
\]
Then
\[
  \Prob(a<X\le b)
  =\Phi\!\left(\frac{b-\mu}{\sigma}\right)
   -\Phi\!\left(\frac{a-\mu}{\sigma}\right).
\]
The density \(\phi\) has no elementary antiderivative, so \(\Phi\) is
evaluated numerically. Symmetry gives \(\Phi(-z)=1-\Phi(z)\), and
\[
  \Prob(|X-\mu|\le\sigma)\approx0.6827,\qquad
  \Prob(|X-\mu|\le1.96\sigma)\approx0.95.
\]
These are probabilities under the assumed distribution, not a claim that
arbitrary data obey the same coverage.

\begin{example}[Measurement error]
Suppose a sensor error \(E\sim\mathcal N(0,0.2^2)\), measured in the sensor's
units. The probability of an absolute error larger than \(0.4\) is
\[
  \Prob(|E|>0.4)=2[1-\Phi(2)]\approx0.0455.
\]
The central \(95\%\) interval is approximately \([-0.392,0.392]\).
\end{example}

\subsection{Transformations and sums}

If \(a\ne0\), then
\[
  aX+b\sim\mathcal N(a\mu+b,a^2\sigma^2).
\]
If \(a=0\), the result is the constant \(b\).
For independent Gaussian variables,
\[
  X\sim\mathcal N(\mu_X,\sigma_X^2),\qquad
  Y\sim\mathcal N(\mu_Y,\sigma_Y^2),
\]
their convolution gives
\[
  X+Y\sim\mathcal N(\mu_X+\mu_Y,\sigma_X^2+\sigma_Y^2).
\]
This conclusion concerns the entire distribution. Linearity of expectation
and additivity of variance identify its parameters, but by themselves do
not prove that the sum is Gaussian.

Many small independent additive effects can produce an approximately
Gaussian aggregate. More precisely, for independent identically distributed
variables with mean \(m\) and finite, nonzero variance \(v\), the central
limit theorem states
\[
  \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n X_i-nm}{\sqrt{nv}}
  \ \xrightarrow{\mathrm d}\ \mathcal N(0,1).
\]
Here convergence in distribution means convergence of CDF values at
continuity points of the limiting CDF. A later chapter develops the theorem
and its limitations. The finite-variance and dependence assumptions matter;
adding arbitrary variables need not produce a Gaussian.

This explains the following useful connections:
\[
  \begin{array}{rcl}
  \operatorname{Binomial}(n,p)
    &\approx& \mathcal N(np,np(1-p)),\\
  \operatorname{Poisson}(m)
    &\approx& \mathcal N(m,m),\\
  \operatorname{Gamma}(k,\lambda)
    &\approx& \mathcal N(k/\lambda,k/\lambda^2).
  \end{array}
\]
The Binomial approximation needs both \(np\) and \(n(1-p)\) to be large;
the Poisson and Gamma approximations improve for large \(m\) and \(k\).
Near a support boundary or far in the tails, these approximations may be
poor. For an integer-valued count, a continuity correction such as
\(\Prob(N\le j)\approx\Phi((j+1/2-m)/\sqrt m)\) accounts for the width of
the probability mass at \(j\).

\section{Mixtures and heterogeneity}
\label{sec:mixtures}

A population may contain several kinds of observations. A measurement can
come from one of several instruments; a request can follow different
execution paths; a randomly selected user can have a different activity
rate from the next user. A mixture describes this two-stage mechanism.

\subsection{First choose a component, then an observation}

Let \(Z\in\{1,\ldots,K\}\) be a component label with
\[
  \Prob(Z=k)=\pi_k,\qquad
  \pi_k\ge0,\qquad \sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k=1.
\]
Given \(Z=k\), generate \(X\) with density \(f_k\). Conditional notation
\(X\mid Z=k\sim f_k\) specifies the distribution inside component \(k\).
The law of total probability gives the marginal density
\begin{equation}
  f_X(x)=\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k f_k(x).
  \label{eq:finite-mixture}
\end{equation}
For discrete components, the identical formula applies to their PMFs.
The label \(Z\) may be unobserved; it is then a \emph{latent variable}.

The weights average \emph{distributions}. They do not mean that each
observation is the weighted sum of one draw from every component.
For example, choosing \(X_1\) or \(X_2\) with equal probability gives a
mixture, whereas \((X_1+X_2)/2\) averages two observations. Those operations
usually produce different distributions.

\subsection{Mean and variance of a finite mixture}

Let component \(k\) have mean \(\mu_k\) and variance \(\sigma_k^2\).
Assume finite second moments. Integrating
\eqref{eq:finite-mixture} gives
\[
  \mu=\Expect[X]=\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k\mu_k,
  \qquad
  \Expect[X^2]
  =\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k(\sigma_k^2+\mu_k^2).
\]
Subtracting \(\mu^2\) and regrouping terms gives
\begin{equation}
  \boxed{
  \Var(X)
  =\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k\sigma_k^2}_{\text{within-component variation}}
   +\underbrace{\sum_{k=1}^K\pi_k(\mu_k-\mu)^2}_{
      \text{between-component variation}}.
  }
  \label{eq:mixture-variance}
\end{equation}
The spread of a pooled population includes variation within its groups and
differences between group means.

In conditional notation these identities are the laws of total expectation
and total variance:
\begin{align}
  \Expect[X]&=\Expect[\Expect[X\mid Z]],\label{eq:total-expectation}\\
  \Var(X)&=\Expect[\Var(X\mid Z)]
       +\Var(\Expect[X\mid Z]).\label{eq:total-variance}
\end{align}
Here \(\Expect[X\mid Z]\) is itself a random variable: its value is
\(\mu_k\) when \(Z=k\). Likewise, \(\Var(X\mid Z)=\sigma_k^2\).
The outer expectation averages over the component label.

\begin{example}[Two sensor settings]
Choose either setting with probability \(1/2\), and suppose
\[
  X\mid Z=1\sim\mathcal N(-2,1),\qquad
  X\mid Z=2\sim\mathcal N(2,1).
\]
The pooled mean is zero. Its variance is
\[
  \Var(X)=\underbrace{1}_{\text{within settings}}
         +\underbrace{4}_{\text{between settings}}=5.
\]
The density has two peaks. The single Gaussian \(\mathcal N(0,5)\) has
the same mean and variance but a different shape
(Figure~\ref{fig:gaussian-mixture}).
For instance, the mixture gives
\[
  \Prob(|X|\le1)=\Phi(3)-\Phi(1)\approx0.1573,
\]
whereas \(\mathcal N(0,5)\) gives
\(2\Phi(1/\sqrt5)-1\approx0.3453\).
Matching two moments does not identify a distribution.
\end{example}

  The dotted curves show the weighted component densities; they add to the

  \label{fig:gaussian-mixture}

If instead \(X_1\sim\mathcal N(-2,1)\) and
\(X_2\sim\mathcal N(2,1)\) are independent and we average them, then
\[
  \frac{X_1+X_2}{2}\sim\mathcal N(0,1/2).
\]
This is neither the mixture nor the Gaussian that matches its moments.

\subsection{A continuous distribution of parameters}

Heterogeneity need not consist of a few categories. Let a parameter
\(\Theta\) have density \(g\), and generate \(X\) conditional on its value.
The marginal density or PMF is
\[
  p_X(x)=\int p(x\mid\theta)g(\theta)\,d\theta.
\]
For continuous \(X\), \(p(x\mid\theta)\) denotes a conditional density;
for discrete \(X\), it denotes a conditional probability mass.
The integral averages over possible parameters. In general, replacing
\(\Theta\) by \(\Expect[\Theta]\) inside \(p(x\mid\Theta)\) does not produce
the same distribution.

Equations~\eqref{eq:total-expectation} and \eqref{eq:total-variance} also
hold for this continuous mixing parameter when the relevant moments exist.
They allow moment calculations without evaluating the full mixture integral.

\subsection{Different users have different event rates}

Suppose the count \(N\) for a randomly chosen user over a fixed observation
window satisfies
\[
  N\mid\Lambda=\lambda\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda).
\]
Here \(\Lambda\) is the user's expected count for that window; all users
are observed over the same duration. Conditional means and variances are
both \(\Lambda\), hence
\[
  \Expect[N]=\Expect[\Lambda],\qquad
  \Var(N)=\Expect[\Lambda]+\Var(\Lambda).
\]
If users differ in their rates, the marginal variance exceeds the mean.
This is \emph{overdispersion} relative to a Poisson model.
It can occur even when each individual user's count is Poisson.
Other mechanisms, such as dependent arrivals, can also cause overdispersion.

Take \(\Lambda\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(\alpha,\beta)\), in the rate
convention. Combining two distributions already studied gives
\[
  \Expect[N]=\frac{\alpha}{\beta},\qquad
  \Var(N)=\frac{\alpha}{\beta}+\frac{\alpha}{\beta^2}.
\]
For completeness, the mixture PMF follows from the gamma integral:
\begin{align*}
  \Prob(N=k)
  &=\int_0^\infty
      \frac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^k}{k!}
      \frac{\beta^\alpha}{\Gamma(\alpha)}
          \lambda^{\alpha-1}e^{-\beta\lambda}\,d\lambda\\
  &=\frac{\Gamma(k+\alpha)}{\Gamma(\alpha)k!}
      \left(\frac{\beta}{\beta+1}\right)^\alpha
      \left(\frac1{\beta+1}\right)^k,\qquad k\ge0.
\end{align*}
This is a \emph{negative binomial distribution}, in a convention that counts
failures and permits any real shape \(\alpha>0\).
Its construction, rather than its name, explains why it is useful for
heterogeneous counts.

For \(\alpha=2,\beta=1/2\), the mean is \(4\) and the variance is \(12\).
A Poisson distribution with mean \(4\) has variance only \(4\).
The heterogeneous model also gives
\[
  \Prob(N=0)=\left(\frac13\right)^2=\frac19\approx0.1111,
\]
compared with \(e^{-4}\approx0.0183\) under the single-rate Poisson model.

\subsection{One random probability or a new one for every trial?}

Let \(P\in[0,1]\) be a random success probability and
\(B\mid P=p\sim\operatorname{Bernoulli}(p)\). Then
\[
  \Prob(B=1)=\Expect[P],
\]
so a single observation is still Bernoulli with parameter
\(q=\Expect[P]\). Heterogeneity cannot be identified from the marginal
distribution of one binary observation alone.

For repeated trials, the sampling mechanism matters. If a user has one
shared probability \(P\) and, conditional on it,
\(S\mid P\sim\operatorname{Binomial}(n,P)\), then
\begin{align*}
  \Expect[S]&=nq,\\
  \Var(S)
   &=n\Expect[P(1-P)]+n^2\Var(P)\\
   &=nq(1-q)+n(n-1)\Var(P).
\end{align*}
If \(n>1\) and \(\Var(P)>0\), the variance exceeds \(nq(1-q)\).
Trials are independent
conditional on the user's probability, but generally dependent after
pooling users with different probabilities.

If instead each trial independently draws a fresh \(P_i\) from the same
distribution and then independently generates \(B_i\mid P_i\), the
unconditional \(B_i\) are independent \(\operatorname{Bernoulli}(q)\),
and their sum is \(\operatorname{Binomial}(n,q)\).
The choice of what is shared across observations is part of the model.

\section{Extension: Gaussian scale mixtures and Student's \texorpdfstring{\(t\)}{t}}
\label{sec:student}

Hidden groups may differ in spread even when they have the same mean.
One simple \emph{contaminated Gaussian} model is
\[
  X\sim (1-\varepsilon)\mathcal N(\mu,\sigma^2)
       +\varepsilon\mathcal N(\mu,a^2\sigma^2),
  \qquad 0<\varepsilon<1,\quad a>1.
\]
A fraction \(\varepsilon\) of observations comes from a noisier component.
The mean remains \(\mu\), while
\[
  \Var(X)=\sigma^2[(1-\varepsilon)+\varepsilon a^2].
\]
Even a small fraction can greatly increase the total variance.

A continuous version randomizes the \emph{precision}, the inverse of
variance. Let
\[
  \tau\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(\nu/2,\nu/2),\qquad
  Z\mid\tau\sim\mathcal N(0,\tau^{-1}),\qquad \nu>0.
\]
Integrating out \(\tau\) gives the standard Student distribution,
\(Z\sim t_\nu\), with density
\[
  f_Z(z)=
  \frac{\Gamma((\nu+1)/2)}
       {\sqrt{\nu\pi}\,\Gamma(\nu/2)}
  \left(1+\frac{z^2}{\nu}\right)^{-(\nu+1)/2}.
\]
Small precisions correspond to occasional large conditional variances.
The resulting tails decay as a power of \(|z|\), much more slowly than
Gaussian tails.

For \(\nu>1\), \(\Expect[Z]=0\); for \(\nu>2\),
\[
  \Var(Z)=\Expect[\tau^{-1}]=\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}.
\]
For \(1<\nu\le2\), the mean exists but the second moment is infinite;
for \(0<\nu\le1\), the mean does not exist. Symmetry alone does not
guarantee a finite mean.

If \(X=\mu+sZ\), where \(s>0\), then \(s\) is a scale parameter,
not the standard deviation: when \(\nu>2\),
\[
  \Var(X)=s^2\frac{\nu}{\nu-2}.
\]
As \(\nu\) grows, the standard Student distribution approaches
\(\mathcal N(0,1)\). It connects Gamma variability, Gaussian conditional
distributions, and heavy-tailed observations in a single model.

\section{Exercises}
\label{sec:distributions-exercises}

\begin{exercise}[Masses, CDF, and moments]
A discrete variable satisfies
\[
  p_X(-1)=a,\qquad p_X(0)=2a,\qquad p_X(2)=a.
\]
Find \(a\), write its CDF, and compute \(\Prob(-1<X\le2)\),
\(\Expect[X]\), and \(\Var(X)\).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A density and a nonlinear expectation]
Let \(f_X(x)=cx\) for \(0\le x\le1\), and zero otherwise.
Find \(c\), derive the CDF, and calculate \(\Prob(0.2<X\le0.7)\).
Find \(\Expect[X]\), \(\Expect[X^2]\), and \(\Var(X)\).
Explain why \(\Expect[X^2]\ne\Expect[X]^2\).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[One arrival process, three distributions]
Requests follow a homogeneous Poisson process at rate \(0.5\) per minute.
Find the probability of no requests in four minutes, the probability that
the third request arrives within four minutes, and the mean and variance
of the waiting time until the third request. Given that no request arrives
in the first six minutes, find the probability of waiting at least two
more minutes for the first request.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Expectation of a function]
For \(T\sim\operatorname{Exp}(\lambda)\) and \(a>0\), find
\(\Expect[e^{-aT}]\) and \(\Var(e^{-aT})\).
For \(\lambda=a=1\), compare \(\Expect[e^{-T}]\) with
\(e^{-\Expect[T]}\).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Averages and dependence]
Let \(X_1,\ldots,X_n\) be independent, with a common mean \(\mu\) and
variance \(\sigma^2\). Find the mean and variance of
\(\overline X=n^{-1}\sum_iX_i\).
What changes if \(X_1=\cdots=X_n=X\) with probability one?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Rare successes and waiting]
Each of \(200\) independent trials succeeds with probability \(0.01\).
Find the count's mean, variance, exact no-success probability, and its
Poisson approximation. In an unlimited sequence of these trials, find the
mean and variance of the number of trials until the first success.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Gaussian sums and standardization]
Independent measurement errors satisfy
\(X\sim\mathcal N(1,4)\) and \(Y\sim\mathcal N(-1,9)\).
Find the distributions of \(X+Y\) and \(2X-Y\).
Express \(\Prob(|X+Y|\le2)\) using \(\Phi\).
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Mixture versus average]
Choose \(X_1\sim\mathcal N(-3,1)\) with probability \(1/4\) and
\(X_2\sim\mathcal N(1,1)\) otherwise; call the selected value \(M\).
Find \(\Expect[M]\) and \(\Var(M)\).
Compare them with the mean and variance of \(A=(X_1+3X_2)/4\),
when \(X_1,X_2\) are independent.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Heterogeneous counts]
For a fixed observation window, let
\(N\mid\Lambda\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\Lambda)\) and
\(\Lambda\sim\operatorname{Gamma}(3,1/2)\).
Find \(\Expect[N]\), \(\Var(N)\), and \(\Prob(N=0)\).
Compare with a Poisson distribution having the same mean.
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[A probability shared across trials]
A user has probability \(P=0.1\) or \(P=0.5\), each with probability \(1/2\).
Conditional on \(P\), their ten binary responses are independent.
Find the mean and variance of the success count.
How does its variance change if a fresh independent \(P_i\) is drawn for
each response instead?
\end{exercise}

\begin{exercise}[Student scale]
Let \(Z\sim t_4\) and \(X=2+3Z\). Find the mean, variance, and standard
deviation of \(X\). What would fail if \(Z\sim t_2\)?
\end{exercise}

\subsection*{Short answers}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \(a=1/4\), with CDF
  \[
    F_X(x)=
    \begin{cases}
      0,&x<-1,\\
      1/4,&-1\le x<0,\\
      3/4,&0\le x<2,\\
      1,&x\ge2.
    \end{cases}
  \]
  Then \(\Prob(-1<X\le2)=3/4\),
  \(\Expect[X]=1/4\), \(\Expect[X^2]=5/4\), and \(\Var(X)=19/16\).

  \item \(c=2\). The CDF is zero for \(x<0\), \(x^2\) on \([0,1]\),
  and one for \(x>1\). The interval probability is \(0.45\).
  Moreover, \(\Expect[X]=2/3\), \(\Expect[X^2]=1/2\), and
  \(\Var(X)=1/18\). Squaring is nonlinear; the difference between the
  two expectations in question is the positive variance.

  \item The no-request probability is \(e^{-2}\approx0.1353\);
  the third-arrival probability is \(1-5e^{-2}\approx0.3233\).
  The third-arrival time has distribution \(\operatorname{Gamma}(3,0.5)\),
  mean \(6\) minutes and variance \(12\) minutes squared.
  Memorylessness gives \(e^{-1}\approx0.3679\) for the additional wait.

  \item \(\Expect[e^{-aT}]=\lambda/(\lambda+a)\) and
  \[
    \Var(e^{-aT})
    =\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+2a}
     -\left(\frac{\lambda}{\lambda+a}\right)^2.
  \]
  At \(\lambda=a=1\), the expectation is \(1/2\), the variance is \(1/12\),
  and \(e^{-\Expect[T]}=e^{-1}\ne1/2\).

  \item In both cases the mean is \(\mu\). The independent average has
  variance \(\sigma^2/n\); the identical-copy average equals \(X\) and has
  variance \(\sigma^2\).

  \item The count has mean \(2\), variance \(1.98\), and no-success
  probability \(0.99^{200}\approx0.1340\); the approximation is
  \(e^{-2}\approx0.1353\).
  The Geometric waiting count has mean \(100\) and variance \(9900\).

  \item \(X+Y\sim\mathcal N(0,13)\),
  \(2X-Y\sim\mathcal N(3,25)\), and the interval probability is
  \(2\Phi(2/\sqrt{13})-1\).

  \item \(\Expect[M]=0\), \(\Var(M)=1+3=4\).
  The average has mean zero and variance
  \((1/4)^2+(3/4)^2=5/8\); in fact \(A\sim\mathcal N(0,5/8)\).

  \item The mean is \(6\), the variance is \(6+12=18\), and
  \(\Prob(N=0)=(1/3)^3=1/27\approx0.0370\).
  A \(\operatorname{Poisson}(6)\) variable has variance \(6\) and
  zero-count probability \(e^{-6}\approx0.00248\).

  \item \(q=\Expect[P]=0.3\) and \(\Var(P)=0.04\).
  The mean is \(3\), and the shared-probability variance is
  \(10(0.3)(0.7)+10(9)(0.04)=5.7\).
  With fresh independent probabilities, the variance is \(2.1\).

  \item \(\Expect[X]=2\), \(\Var(X)=9\cdot4/(4-2)=18\), and
  \(\operatorname{sd}(X)=3\sqrt2\).
  For \(t_2\), the mean still exists but the variance is infinite.
\end{enumerate}
